Crossing the Bar
— Alfred Lord Tennyson
Sunset and evening star, And one clear call for me! And may there be no moaning of the bar, When I put out to sea,
But such a tide as moving seems asleep, Too full for sound and foam, When that which drew from out the boundless deep Turns again home.
Twilight and evening bell, And after that the dark! And may there be no sadness of farewell, When I embark;
For tho' from out our bourne of Time and Place The flood may bear me far, I hope to see my Pilot face to face When I have crost the bar.

Overall Symbolic Interpretation
This poem is an extended metaphor (रूपक) for death as a sea voyage. The poet Tennyson, old and near death when he wrote this, uses the image of a ship sailing out of a harbour into the open sea to describe the soul leaving the mortal world and journeying to God.
• The harbour = life on earth
• Crossing the bar = the moment of death
• The open sea = the afterlife/eternity
• The Pilot = God, whom the speaker hopes to meet after death
The poem expresses calm acceptance of death — not fear, not grief. The speaker wishes for a quiet, peaceful death with no mourning, trusting that God (the Pilot) will receive him on the other side.

Teacher's Speech Notes for the Class
Read along as the teacher introduces the poem
"Students, have you ever stood at the edge of a river or a sea and wondered what lies beyond? Today's poem is about exactly that — but the poet is not talking about water. He is talking about death. Let us read together."
Background on Poet: Alfred Lord Tennyson (1809–1892) was a famous English poet, Poet Laureate of Britain. He wrote this poem in 1889, when he was 80 years old, just three years before his death. He asked that this poem always be placed last in any collection of his works — because it was his farewell to the world.
Setting the Context: A bar is a sandbar — a ridge of sand at the mouth of a harbour. When a ship sails out of the harbour into the open sea, it must cross the bar. This is the central image of the poem. Tennyson uses this image as a metaphor for death.
Key Teaching Points:

1. Tone — The tone is calm, peaceful, accepting. The speaker does NOT fear death. Notice — he says "may there be no moaning... no sadness of farewell." He does not want mourning. He is ready.

2. Structure — 4 stanzas of 4 lines each. Rhyme scheme: ABAB. Find the rhyming words.

3. The Pilot — Who do you think the Pilot is? It is God.

4. Universal Theme — In our culture too, we believe that after death, the soul goes to a better place. Tennyson believes the same — he calls that place "home."
Closing Discussion Question: "Do you think a person can face death without fear? What gives people courage at the end of life?"

Line-by-Line Explanation
Each stanza explained in detail
Stanza 1
"Sunset and evening star, / And one clear call for me!"
The poem opens at sunset — a symbol of the end of life. The "evening star" appears as light fades. The poet hears "one clear call" — the call of death, clear and certain. He knows his time has come.
"And may there be no moaning of the bar, / When I put out to sea,"
"Moaning of the bar" = the loud, crashing sound of waves hitting the sandbar. Symbolically: no loud grief or mourning when he dies. "Put out to sea" = sail away = die. He wishes for a quiet, undisturbed departure.
Stanza 2
"But such a tide as moving seems asleep, / Too full for sound and foam,"
He wants a calm, full tide — so deep and full that it moves silently, without foam or noise. Symbolically: a peaceful, quiet death — no drama, no suffering.
"When that which drew from out the boundless deep / Turns again home."
"That which drew from out the boundless deep" = his soul, which originally came from God (the infinite deep) and is now returning home to God. Life came from God; at death, it returns to God.
Stanza 3
"Twilight and evening bell, / And after that the dark!"
Twilight = the last faint light before darkness — symbolises the very last moments of life. The evening bell rings — like a church bell signalling day's end, here it signals the end of life. Then dark = death.
"And may there be no sadness of farewell, / When I embark;"
Again the speaker asks: no sad goodbyes. "Embark" = board a vessel and set off. He begins his final journey calmly and willingly.
Stanza 4
"For tho' from out our bourne of Time and Place / The flood may bear me far,"
"Bourne of Time and Place" = the boundary of our mortal world — time and physical space. The "flood" (the tide/death) will carry him far beyond this world. He will leave everything earthly behind.
"I hope to see my Pilot face to face / When I have crost the bar."
The most important lines of the poem. After crossing the boundary of death, the speaker hopes to meet God face to face. The "Pilot" (God) has been guiding his life's journey all along — and now, at last, he will see him directly. This is a statement of faith and hope, not fear.

Exercise B — Summary Completion (Fill in the Gaps)
Click each blank to reveal the correct word
The speaker is making a journey with her guide. She asks the guide eight different questions about the road ahead. The first question is if the road is all up-hill and if the journey will take all day. The guide replies in the affirmative. Next, she asks if there is a place to rest for the night. The guide informs the speaker that there is an inn which she won't miss. The speaker's fifth question is, whether she will meet the other travellers on the way. At the inn, the speaker asks if she should knock or call the fellow travellers. The guide replies that someone will open the door. Lastly, the speaker asks if there will be a bed for her. The guide tells her that there are beds for everyone.
