📜 Rickshaw Ride — The Poem

Stanza I That man by the harbour Spitting into the water Is as old as China.
Stanza II He is made of leather And his folded face Is like a sandbar At low tide.
Stanza III Will you take a ride On his rickshaw ? He will not thank you For your dollar, He knows that to you A dollar does not matter.
Stanza IV Off you go with a clatter Up to Victoria On up the long hill Pass the cathedral To the peak Tram Pull! Pull!
Stanza V He leans to it His muscles at neck and calf and shoulder And his dark veins that snake Take your eye Not what passes by.
Stanza VI Let him not break Something for my sake, For my dollar.
Stanza VII You went to Hong Kong They will say. Did you take a rickshaw? Was it fun? Did he run, your rickshaw man? Yes, he ran. No, it was not much fun.
— John Hill

🎭 Symbolic Words and Phrases

Symbol / PhraseSymbolic Meaning
"Is as old as China" Represents extreme old age; China symbolizes one of the oldest civilizations, suggesting the rickshaw man is very old and worn out.
"He is made of leather" Symbolizes toughness and hardness. The rickshaw man's skin is rough and dark like leather due to years of hard labor under the sun.
"His folded face is like a sandbar at low tide" His wrinkled face is compared to a sandbar — just as a sandbar appears when water is low, his wrinkles appear because his life's energy (water) has dried up.
"Veins that snake" His protruding veins, twisted like snakes, symbolize extreme physical exertion and poverty-driven hard work.
"For my dollar" The dollar symbolizes the guilt and conscience of the rich rider. The small fare represents the huge economic gap between the rider and the puller.
"Pull! Pull!" Symbolizes the relentless struggle of the poor and the working class who are pushed to their limits for the comfort of the wealthy.
"Let him not break something for my sake" Symbolizes the rider's guilt and empathy — he fears the old man might injure himself pulling him up the steep hill.
"No, it was not much fun" This symbolic ending shows that the ride was not enjoyable because the rider was deeply moved by the old man's suffering and labor.

📖 Symbolic Interpretation of the Poem

"Rickshaw Ride" — A Social Commentary

"Rickshaw Ride" by John Hill is a poem that goes far beyond describing a simple rickshaw journey in Hong Kong. It is a social commentary on the exploitation of the poor by the wealthy. The rickshaw puller — old, worn, muscular, and wrinkled — represents millions of laborers across the world who toil endlessly for minimal payment. The rider, representing the privileged class, is troubled by guilt as he watches the old man strain every muscle to carry him uphill.

The poem symbolically contrasts wealth and poverty, comfort and suffering, the observer and the observed. The final lines — "No, it was not much fun" — deliver a powerful message: true enjoyment cannot come at the cost of another person's suffering. The poem invites readers to question social inequality and reflect on how they treat those who serve them.

🎓 Teacher's Speech Notes

Introduction

"Good morning, students. Today we are going to read and enjoy a very thoughtful poem called Rickshaw Ride written by John Hill. This poem is set in Hong Kong and describes a tourist's experience of riding a rickshaw pulled by a very old man. But this poem is much more than just a journey — it carries a very deep message about society, poverty, and human dignity. Let's explore it together."

Setting the Context

"Imagine you are in Hong Kong, a busy city. You see an old man sitting near the harbour, waiting for passengers. He is very old, his face full of wrinkles, his veins visible on his arms. Someone hires him to pull a rickshaw up a steep hill to a famous place called Victoria Peak. As you read this poem, think about how you would feel sitting in that rickshaw."

Guiding Questions for Students
  • "How does the poet describe the old man's appearance?"
  • "What does the phrase 'He is made of leather' mean to you?"
  • "Why does the rider say 'Let him not break something for my sake'?"
  • "Did the rider enjoy the ride? Why or why not?"
  • "What message do you think the poet is giving us?"
Conclusion

"This poem teaches us to be sensitive to the hardships of others. The next time someone does a difficult job for us — whether it's a porter, a cleaner, or a laborer — let us recognize their effort and treat them with respect and dignity."

🔍 Line by Line Explanation

"That man by the harbour / Spitting into the water / Is as old as China." ▶
The poet introduces the rickshaw puller. He is standing by the harbour (seaport), spitting casually into the water. The comparison "as old as China" tells us he is extremely old. China is one of the world's oldest civilizations, so this simile suggests the man is ancient and worn out by time.
"He is made of leather / And his folded face / Is like a sandbar at low tide." ▶
The old man's skin is compared to leather — tough, dark, and wrinkled from years of exposure to the sun. His wrinkled face is like a sandbar at low tide — just as a sandbar is exposed and dry when the tide is low, the man's face is exposed and weathered by age and hard labor.
"Will you take a ride / On his rickshaw? / He will not thank you for your dollar..." ▶
The poet asks the reader if they would hire this old man. The old man will not be grateful for the dollar because he knows that for the rider, a dollar is nothing — it is a trivial amount. This highlights the economic gap between the wealthy rider and the poor puller.
"Off you go with a clatter / Up to Victoria / ... To the peak Tram / Pull! Pull!" ▶
The journey begins with the clatter of the rickshaw wheels. The route goes uphill to Victoria Peak, passing a cathedral, heading to the Peak Tram station. "Pull! Pull!" captures the intense effort the old man must make to drag the rickshaw uphill.
"He leans to it / His muscles at neck and calf and shoulder..." ▶
The rider's attention is completely captured by the old man's strained body — the tight muscles of his neck, calf, and shoulders, and the dark veins twisting under his skin like snakes. The rider cannot enjoy the scenery because he is too focused on the old man's physical struggle.
"Let him not break / Something for my sake, / For my dollar." ▶
The rider feels guilty. He prays that the old man does not injure himself — break a muscle or collapse — just to earn the rider's dollar. This shows deep empathy and guilt.
"You went to Hong Kong / ... Did he run, your rickshaw man? / Yes, he ran. / No, it was not much fun." ▶
Back home, people ask about the trip. They assume it must have been fun — a typical tourist experience. But the rider's honest answer is: "No, it was not much fun." The experience of watching an old, struggling man labor for his comfort made the ride emotionally painful rather than enjoyable.

✅ Exercise C — Answer the Following Questions

a. How old is the rickshaw puller? ▶
The rickshaw puller is extremely old — the poet compares him to China, one of the world's oldest civilizations, suggesting he is very aged and worn out.
b. What does 'he is made of leather' mean? ▶
It means the rickshaw puller's skin is very tough, dark, and wrinkled — like leather — due to years of hard work and exposure to the sun. It symbolizes his rough and hardened life.
c. What attracts the attention of the rider? ▶
The rider's attention is attracted by the old man's straining muscles at the neck, calf, and shoulder, and his dark, twisted veins that stand out under the skin as he pulls the rickshaw uphill.
d. Why does the rider want the rickshaw puller not to fall? ▶
The rider feels guilty and empathetic. He fears that the old man might injure or collapse himself — "break something" — just to earn the small dollar fare. He does not want the man to suffer for his benefit.
e. Did the man enjoy the rickshaw ride? Give reasons. ▶
No, the man did not enjoy the rickshaw ride. Despite it being a tourist experience, watching the old man strain every muscle and struggle up the hill made the rider feel guilty and sad rather than happy.

➕ Additional Short Questions

1. Where does the poet first see the rickshaw puller? ▶
The poet first sees the rickshaw puller by the harbour (the seaport/coast of Hong Kong), where he is sitting and spitting into the water, waiting for customers.
2. Why does the poet say the old man's face is like a sandbar at low tide? ▶
Just as a sandbar becomes visible and dry when the sea water recedes at low tide, the old man's wrinkled face shows the drying up of his life's energy — his youth and strength have been worn away by age and hardship.
3. What route does the rickshaw take in the poem? ▶
The rickshaw goes with a clatter up to Victoria, on up the long hill, past the cathedral, and finally to the Peak Tram station at Victoria Peak in Hong Kong.
4. What does the expression "Pull! Pull!" suggest? ▶
"Pull! Pull!" suggests the intense physical effort required by the old man to drag the rickshaw up the steep hill. It symbolizes the relentless struggle of the poor working class for the comfort of the wealthy.
5. What is the overall message of the poem "Rickshaw Ride"? ▶
The poem's message is that true enjoyment cannot come at the cost of another person's suffering. It calls attention to social inequality and urges readers to treat all working people with empathy, respect, and dignity.

➕ True / False

1. The rickshaw puller is a young and energetic man. ▶
❌ False — The rickshaw puller is described as extremely old, "as old as China," with a wrinkled, leather-like face.
2. The rider feels guilty watching the old man struggle. ▶
✅ True — The rider prays "Let him not break something for my sake," showing clear guilt and empathy.
3. The rider thoroughly enjoyed the rickshaw ride. ▶
❌ False — The rider explicitly says, "No, it was not much fun." Watching the old man suffer ruined the experience.
4. The old man's veins are compared to snakes in the poem. ▶
✅ True — The poet says "his dark veins that snake," comparing the twisting veins to snakes.
5. The dollar is a very important amount of money for the rider. ▶
❌ False — To the wealthy rider, "a dollar does not matter." It is a trivial amount, highlighting the economic gap.
