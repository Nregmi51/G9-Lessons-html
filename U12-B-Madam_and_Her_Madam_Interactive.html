📜 The Poem — Madam and Her Madam

Madam and Her Madam
— Langston Hughes
Stanza 1
I worked for a woman,
She wasn't mean,
But she had a twelve-room
House to clean.
Stanza 2
Had to get breakfast,
Dinner,
and supper too
Then take care of her children
When I got through.
Stanza 3
Wash, iron, and scrub,
Walk the dog around
It was too much,
Nearly broke me down.
Stanza 4
I said, Madam,
Can it be
You trying to make a
Pack-horse out of me?
Stanza 5
She opened her mouth.
She cried, Oh, no!
You know, Alberta,
I love you so!
Stanza 6
I said, Madam,
That may be true
But I'll be dogged
If I love you!

🕊️ Symbolic / Thematic Interpretation

Class Inequality & Labour Exploitation

The poem uses the relationship between a domestic worker (the maid, Alberta) and her employer (the Madam) to symbolise class inequality and labour exploitation. The Madam's false declaration of love — "I love you so!" — contrasts sharply with the exhausting workload she assigns. The poem symbolically represents how the oppressed are silenced with sweet words while being overworked. Alberta's final retort — "I'll be dogged if I love you!" — is a symbol of reclaiming dignity and speaking truth to power.

👩‍🏫 Teacher's Speech Notes

🎤 Introduction "Today we are going to read a poem by Langston Hughes — an African-American poet famous for writing about the lives of working-class Black people in America. This poem tells the story of a domestic worker named Alberta and her employer. As we read, think: is this a poem about work, or is it about something deeper?"
📋 Setting Context "Langston Hughes wrote during the Harlem Renaissance period in early 20th century America. Many Black women worked as domestic servants in white households. They were often overworked and underpaid. This poem reflects their real experience."
📖 Reading Guidance "Notice how simple the language is — short lines, everyday words. But the meaning is very deep. The maid does all the housework — breakfast, dinner, supper, washing, ironing, scrubbing, walking the dog, and caring for children. How many tasks is that? Count them with your students."
🔑 Key Teaching Points 1. Irony in "She wasn't mean" — she WAS cruel through overwork
2. The pack-horse metaphor — dehumanisation of the worker
3. The Madam's empty love declaration — false kindness
4. Alberta's final line — courage, self-respect, humour
💬 Discussion Prompt "Do you think the maid was wrong to speak back to her employer? What does it take to stand up for yourself at work?"
🎓 Closing "This poem teaches us that dignity is more important than politeness. Alberta doesn't shout — she speaks clearly and honestly. That is a form of courage."

📝 Line-by-Line Explanation

"I worked for a woman, / She wasn't mean"
The speaker (Alberta, the maid) introduces her employer. She says the woman wasn't cruel by nature — but the following lines reveal that her workload WAS cruel. This opening line is ironic.
"But she had a twelve-room / House to clean."
A twelve-room house is enormous. The word "but" signals contradiction — though the Madam wasn't "mean," she assigned an almost impossible workload. One person cannot easily maintain twelve rooms.
"Had to get breakfast, / Dinner, and supper, too / Then take care of her children / When I got through."
Alberta had to cook three meals a day AND look after the children after completing all her work. "When I got through" means after finishing all other tasks — leaving no rest.
"Wash, iron, and scrub, / Walk the dog around"
Three more hard physical tasks listed rapidly — laundry, pressing clothes, intensive cleaning, and even walking the pet. The listing pace mimics exhaustion.
"It was too much, / Nearly broke me down."
The maid reaches her limit. "Broke me down" means she was physically and emotionally exhausted. "Nearly" suggests she survived, but barely.
"I said, Madam, / Can it be / You trying to make a / Pack-horse out of me?"
Alberta finally speaks up. She uses the powerful metaphor of a pack-horse — an animal used purely for heavy work. She is asking: "Do you see me as human or as a beast of burden?"
"She opened her mouth. / She cried, Oh, no! / You know, Alberta, / I love you so!"
The Madam's response is emotional and deflecting. Instead of acknowledging the overwork, she says "I love you." This is a typical strategy of the powerful — using affection to avoid accountability.
"I said, Madam, / That may be true / But I'll be dogged / If I love you!"
Alberta doesn't deny the Madam's love but refuses to return it. "I'll be dogged" means "I'll be stubborn — I absolutely will NOT." This is the poem's emotional climax. It is funny, brave, and deeply honest. Alberta reclaims her dignity.

B. Answer the following questions.

a. Was the Madam really not mean? Why does the speaker say 'she wasn't mean'? ▶
The Madam was effectively cruel in her actions even if not by intention. The speaker says "she wasn't mean" ironically — the Madam may have been pleasant in manner, but she gave Alberta an overwhelming list of duties. The statement reflects how oppressed people sometimes make excuses for their oppressors or soften criticism.
b. Make a list of work that the maid had to do. ▶
The maid had to:
  1. Cook breakfast
  2. Cook dinner (main meal)
  3. Cook supper (evening meal)
  4. Take care of the Madam's children
  5. Wash clothes
  6. Iron clothes
  7. Scrub (clean the house)
  8. Walk the dog
c. What made her nearly break down? ▶
The enormous workload — cooking three meals a day, caring for children, washing, ironing, scrubbing, and walking the dog — in a twelve-room house was far too much for one person. This excessive burden nearly caused her physical and emotional collapse.
d. How did the Madam respond to the maid's question? ▶
Instead of admitting that she had overloaded the maid with work, the Madam cried out emotionally — "Oh, no! You know, Alberta, I love you so!" She used declarations of love to avoid addressing the real problem. This response was evasive and emotionally manipulative.

➕ Additional Practice Questions

Short Questions

1. Who is the speaker in the poem "Madam and Her Madam"? ▶
The speaker is Alberta, a domestic worker (maid).
2. What does the phrase "pack-horse" suggest about how the maid was treated? ▶
It suggests she was treated like an animal used only for carrying heavy loads — overworked and dehumanised.
3. How many meals did the maid have to prepare every day? ▶
She had to prepare three meals: breakfast, dinner, and supper.
4. Why does Alberta say "I'll be dogged if I love you"? ▶
Alberta says this to express her refusal to love someone who exploits her, despite whatever affection the Madam claims to feel. It is an act of self-respect.
5. What is the central theme of the poem? ▶
The central theme is labour exploitation, class inequality, and the dignity of the working class.

True / False

1. The Madam had a ten-room house to clean. ▶
False — The Madam had a twelve-room house, not a ten-room house.
2. Alberta had to walk the dog as part of her duties. ▶
True — Walking the dog was one of Alberta's many daily tasks.
3. The Madam admitted she was giving Alberta too much work. ▶
False — The Madam did not admit it; she said "I love you" instead of addressing the workload.
4. Alberta's hard work nearly broke her down. ▶
True — The poem states "It was too much, nearly broke me down."
5. Alberta loved the Madam deeply by the end of the poem. ▶
False — Alberta said "I'll be dogged if I love you!" — she firmly refused to love the Madam.
